What this note follows

The question is not simply whether ESPRESSO keeps a line still. It is whether different calibration sources assign the same wavelength to the same detector location. This reading follows the authors’ calibration comparison, the non-parametric instrumental line-spread function (LSF), and the residual disagreement that survives a better model.

The connection to my instrumentation work is methodological: centroid motion, line shape, extraction, and wavelength assignment should be checked separately. This note does not claim that a calibration discrepancy is an equivalent stellar radial-velocity error.

Source findings · see the pinned manuscript

What the authors report

Using January 2023 calibration exposures, Schmidt and Bouchy infer spatially varying, asymmetric ESPRESSO LSFs from unresolved laser-frequency-comb lines. Re-fitting calibration spectra with those profiles reduces the discrepancy between ThAr/Fabry–Pérot and comb wavelength solutions from roughly 50 m/s to about 10 m/s peak-to-valley over most of the covered range, excluding the problematic reddest orders. Fibres and slices become much more consistent. A global offset, red-end structure, and fast line-to-line “beat” residuals remain. This is an accuracy study, not a demonstration of 10 cm/s time-series stability.

Interpretation and technical reasoning

Precision, accuracy, and stability are different questions

A narrow distribution can be precisely wrong. Repeatability measures scatter; accuracy concerns bias against a reference; stability asks how a calibration changes with time. Those distinctions prevent an impressive short-run detector RMS from being advertised as absolute wavelength accuracy.

For a control experiment, it is useful to record both the variable being stabilised and the later scientific estimator. A line can retain its centre while its wings change, or its fitted centre can move solely because the assumed profile is wrong. Mechanical and analysis error budgets therefore meet at the image model.

Interpretation and technical reasoning

Why a Gaussian can move the answer

An LSF describes how an intrinsically narrow input is spread across the extracted spectrum. An asymmetric response need not have its peak, first moment, and best-fitting Gaussian centre at the same position. The important error is not a visibly dramatic deformation; it is a small centroid bias relative to the precision being sought.

Calibration sources are not interchangeable probes. Comb lines are effectively unresolved, while the Fabry–Pérot lines have appreciable intrinsic width. Convolving those different inputs with the same asymmetric response can produce different Gaussian-fit biases. A correct instrumental model is useful precisely because it connects the source to the observed pixels.

Interpretation and technical reasoning

Non-parametric does not mean assumption-free

The authors split orders into blocks and infer a finely sampled LSF with a Gaussian-process smoothness prior. The data alone do not constrain every fine-grid value independently. This is a regularised inverse problem, not a direct photograph of the optical response.

A sensible reading asks which properties are supported by repeated line samples, which are enforced by smoothness, and which are sensitive to background subtraction. Making the model more flexible is not automatically an improvement: it can fit noise or hide errors in the assumed intrinsic spectrum.

Connection to my work

EXOhSPEC control work provides a reason to care about detector motion, environmental coupling, and residual structure. This paper suggests retaining line widths, asymmetries, per-line positions, and extraction diagnostics alongside a common displacement estimate. A single “stable image” number is a useful engineering result, but does not close a spectroscopic calibration budget.

The transferable lesson is to test a pipeline with independent information. A second source, another fibre, or a different line subset is more revealing than tuning the same residual repeatedly. These are proposed applications, not a claim that I reproduced the ESPRESSO analysis.

Proposed work · not performed

Tests I would run next

Hold out selected comb lines when constructing the LSF and predict them afterward. Compare performance at different detector positions and signal levels rather than reporting only an all-order average. This would test interpolation and sensitivity to local calibration coverage.

For a laboratory spectrograph, inject controlled sub-pixel shifts and compare centroid estimators with profile-aware fits. Keep raw frames, extraction settings, background models, and uncertainty definitions. A repeatability test should include changes in illumination and line intensity, not only additional frames at one setting.

Extended reading · methods, assumptions and interpretation

Following the full technical argument

Following the background-to-LSF inference

A diffuse continuum and broad overlapping line wings can both raise the inter-line flux. Estimating the background first simplifies the fit, but the resulting LSF inherits that decision. This is especially important where neighbouring lines are close in units of the instrumental resolution.

The practical diagnostic is to examine the far wings and the residual spectrum together. A small core residual can coexist with a biased background. Conversely, a better global residual can conceal an unphysical profile. Physical plausibility and predictive performance should both be checked.

Pixel sampling is information, not just resolution

A finely sampled computational grid cannot create new detector information. Several unresolved lines can improve the LSF estimate when their centres fall at different pixel phases, but repeated lines at redundant phases contribute less independent structure than their number suggests.

A tunable comb would help separate optical response from sampling effects by moving known lines across pixel boundaries. This explains why the paper’s instrumental recommendations accompany its statistical model: software flexibility alone cannot repair an uninformative calibration experiment.

What the remaining disagreement teaches

The before-and-after comparison is persuasive because several fibre and slice patterns disappear together. It is not a complete solution: the remaining wavelength dependence and the fast residual modulation show that a single average profile per block is not the whole measurement chain.

For downstream science, keeping order, fibre, and slice identity preserves the relation to the local LSF. Prematurely co-adding everything can discard information needed for forward modelling. That is a general data-management lesson as well as a specialist calibration issue.
