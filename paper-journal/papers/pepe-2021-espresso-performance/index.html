ESPRESSO@VLT: On-Sky Performance and First Results

A close reading of Pepe et al. (2021) as a benchmark for fibre-fed, vacuum-stabilised extreme-precision radial-velocity spectrographs, with emphasis on thermal architecture, scrambling, calibration strategy, and what remains unquantified.

Why I read this paper

I read this paper because ESPRESSO is one of the clearest reference cases for what a modern optical EPRV spectrograph has to control at the same time: thermal environment, pressure, fibre illumination, calibration source delivery, detector sampling, and operational stability. I am not treating the paper as a trophy instrument description. I am using it as a design baseline for asking which parts of a stability architecture can be copied, which parts must be measured locally, and which assumptions become risky when one moves away from a full vacuum enclosure.

That distinction matters for my EXOhSPEC work. A compact spectrograph with active environmental control cannot simply claim the stability philosophy of ESPRESSO. It has to demonstrate where the environmental disturbance enters, how it propagates to optical path length or image motion, and whether the feedback system is acting on the variable that actually limits the spectrum.

Core result and quantitative baseline

ESPRESSO is installed at the VLT Combined Coudé focus and can operate with any one of the four Unit Telescopes or with all four Unit Telescopes together. Pepe et al. report resolving powers of about R ≈ 140,000 and R ≈ 190,000 in 1-UT modes over 378.2–788.7 nm, and R ≈ 70,000 in 4-UT mode, where the collecting area is equivalent to a 16 m telescope.[1]

The performance numbers are important because they connect the hardware architecture to on-sky use. After the final configuration following fibre upgrade work, the paper reports overall optical throughput above 10% at 550 nm under nominal 0.65 arcsec seeing, radial-velocity precision better than 25 cm/s over one night, and better than 50 cm/s over several months. The authors state that the demonstrated values are limited by photon noise and stellar jitter and are compatible with a 10 cm/s instrumental precision floor.[1]

Instrument architecture: what has to be stabilised

The spectrograph is placed inside a large vacuum vessel in the Combined Coudé Laboratory. The thermal design uses a multi-shell active enclosure, with the goal of attenuating room-level thermal variation before it reaches the optical bench. The note I take from this is not simply “vacuum is better”. The more useful point is that ESPRESSO is designed as a cascade: room control, thermal shells, vacuum vessel, optical bench, fibre scrambling, calibration delivery, and data reduction all contribute to suppressing different classes of error.

The fibre feed uses octagonal fibres and a double scrambler positioned at the vessel feed-through. This matters because a spectrograph calibrated at the cm/s level is sensitive not only to where the spectral lines fall in wavelength, but to how the near-field and far-field illumination fill the fibre and pupil. The paper describes the architecture intended to homogenise the illumination; a separate quantitative residual-illumination-stability budget is not the main focus of the performance paper.

The calibration scheme described in the paper is also deliberately pragmatic. ThAr provides absolute wavelength anchoring, while a white-light Fabry-Pérot etalon provides dense drift monitoring. The laser frequency comb was intended as a more complete calibration reference, but at the time of the paper it was not yet reliable or fully integrated into routine operation.[1]

Key concept: thermal stability as a cascade

The thermal design should be read as a transfer-function problem. A kelvin-level room disturbance is not expected to be cancelled by a single high-gain loop acting directly on the optical bench. Instead, the room, enclosure shells and vacuum vessel form a sequence of low-pass and actively controlled stages. Each layer reduces the disturbance seen by the next layer.

This framing is more useful than quoting one steady-state RMS value. The same RMS temperature stability can have very different consequences depending on where the spectral power sits. Residual thermal variation at minute timescales can contribute to intra-exposure wavelength drift; residual variation at multi-day timescales behaves more like a slowly varying background trend. A paper can report mK-level stability and still leave open the question of where the remaining thermal noise lives in frequency space.

For EXOhSPEC, the immediate lesson is to measure the thermal response chain. The relevant question is not only “how stable is the temperature?” but “which environmental modes reach the optical path, on which timescales, and with what phase lag?”

Open technical questions after reading

  • Thermal transfer function. What is the frequency-domain transfer function from room to each enclosure shell, vessel boundary and optical bench? A steady-state number alone does not tell us whether the residual power matters during an exposure, a night, or a season.
  • Fibre-link failure mode. The paper reports a fibre-link throughput deficiency that required repair and reinstallation. The generic phrase “fibre link” does not identify whether the dominant issue was connector alignment, coating degradation, modal behaviour, stress, or another mechanical/optical factor.
  • Calibration injection repeatability. The paper describes the calibration architecture, but later ESPRESSO EOM-comb work found local wavelength-solution discrepancies up to 15 m/s associated with calibration-unit misalignment, not the comb sources themselves.[2] That result makes the repeatability of the injection geometry a central question.
  • Scrambling residuals. The double scrambler is designed to stabilise illumination, but the public performance paper is not primarily a quantitative residual-pupil-stability study. At the cm/s level, the tolerance on residual illumination changes is severe.
  • 4-UT stability. The 4-UT mode is scientifically valuable, but coherent delivery of four telescope beams through the Coudé train can introduce differential optical-path and illumination terms that are not identical to 1-UT operation.
  • ThAr + Fabry-Pérot limitations. ThAr gives absolute anchors and the Fabry-Pérot gives dense coverage, but later work on broadband Fabry-Pérot etalons shows that FP modes can drift chromatically rather than as a single rigid grid.[3] That makes the combined wavelength solution worth re-testing with an absolute comb reference.

How I would test or extend this

  • Thermal response experiment: place high-precision temperature sensors at the room, shell, vessel and bench levels and record simultaneously for several weeks. Cross-spectral analysis would show which environmental frequencies propagate to the bench.
  • Calibration-injection monitor: use a back-illuminated camera or a dedicated fibre-head monitoring mode to measure calibration-source position and angle across repeated switching cycles. The aim is to separate stable source metrology from delivered calibration geometry.
  • Fabry-Pérot / ThAr / LFC cross-check: use occasional LFC or EOM-comb exposures to anchor the Fabry-Pérot drift model and test whether ThAr absolute anchors plus a drifting FP grid produce wavelength-dependent residuals.
  • 4-UT baseline programme: observe the same quiet star repeatedly in 4-UT mode over several months and compare the residuals against a 1-UT baseline. This would isolate additional systematics from multi-UT optical delivery.

Connection to EXOhSPEC and my work

The ESPRESSO paper gives me a checklist rather than a recipe. I should be able to say where the environmental disturbance enters, how strongly each layer attenuates it, how the fibre feed changes illumination stability, and where the wavelength solution remains vulnerable. If I cannot answer those questions for EXOhSPEC, then the feedback loop is not yet fully characterised.

It also changes how I should write about TEC feedback. A TEC loop should not be presented as an isolated control trick. It should be described as one layer inside a larger stability architecture that includes optical path length monitoring, environmental sensing, centroid or echellogram tracking, and calibration strategy. That is the difference between a control demonstration and an instrument-stability argument.

Overall reading

This paper is best treated as a performance-validation document for a mature EPRV instrument. Its importance comes from the combination of measured on-sky performance and the engineering chain behind it: multi-shell thermal control, vacuum operation, fibre scrambling, calibration delivery, and the reporting of practical limitations such as fibre throughput and LFC integration. The useful lesson is not that every future instrument must be ESPRESSO-shaped. The lesson is that cm/s-level work requires several coupled subsystems to be measured together, with explicit attention to what still leaks through each stability layer.
