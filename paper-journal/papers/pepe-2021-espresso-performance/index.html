Why this is a targeted reading note, not a complete instrument review

This page is not meant to be an exhaustive line-by-line review of the entire ESPRESSO instrument paper. The paper spans front-end optics, fibre feed, spectrograph design, calibration, commissioning, throughput, observing modes, and radial-velocity performance. The reading here is narrower: it follows the parts most relevant to how a precision spectrograph converts environmental and optical disturbances into a controlled stability hierarchy.

That is the more professional way to state the limitation. Instead of saying the paper has not been read thoroughly, the page makes clear which sections are being read closely and why. The open questions are therefore part of the analysis: they identify where the paper provides enough evidence to make a technical inference, but not enough to close the problem completely.

What ESPRESSO is designed to solve

ESPRESSO is the Echelle SPectrograph for Rocky Exoplanets and Stable Spectroscopic Observations. Its role is not simply to collect high-resolution spectra, but to make spectra stable enough for extreme-precision radial velocity measurements. In 1-UT High Resolution mode, the paper reports resolving power around R ≈ 140,000 over 378.2–788.7 nm; the Ultra High Resolution mode reaches approximately R ≈ 190,000; and the 4-UT mode provides an equivalent 16 m aperture at approximately R ≈ 70,000.[1]

The performance numbers define the scale of the engineering problem. The paper reports throughput above 10% near 550 nm under nominal 0.65 arcsec seeing, radial-velocity precision better than 25 cm/s over one night, and better than 50 cm/s over several months. The demonstrated values are described as limited by photon noise and stellar jitter, consistent with a 10 cm/s instrumental precision floor.[1]

Coudé train: the instrument begins before the spectrograph bench

ESPRESSO receives light from the VLT Unit Telescopes through the Coudé train before the beam reaches the Combined Coudé Laboratory. The front-end module includes atmospheric-dispersion correction, field and pupil stabilisation, and calibration-light injection. That means the telescope-to-lab optical path is not just delivery hardware; it is part of the instrument.

This matters for EXOhSPEC-style thinking because detector motion or wavelength drift cannot automatically be blamed on the bench. It may originate in input illumination, pupil motion, fibre injection, calibration geometry, pressure and temperature changes, or a coupled combination of these effects.

Fibre feed, pseudo-slit, and scrambling

The ESPRESSO fibre feed uses octagonal fibres and a double scrambler. The paper describes the scrambler as exchanging near-field and far-field illumination so guiding residuals and pupil wobble are suppressed before they become spectral-format changes. The design lesson is clear: a stable wavelength solution is not sufficient if the light enters the spectrograph in a different spatial state from exposure to exposure.

The pseudo-slit and spectral-format figures make this visible. HR, UHR, and MR modes map fibre outputs differently onto the detector. Any residual near-field or far-field change can alter line shapes, extraction weights, or order-dependent centroids. In radial-velocity terms, the fibre feed is part of the velocity measurement, not a passive pipe.

The useful question is not only whether the fibre feed scrambles light well. It is whether the remaining illumination variation is small enough for the claimed RV floor, and whether that residual was measured under real observing conditions.

Vacuum and multi-shell thermal stability

ESPRESSO is placed inside a large vacuum vessel in the Combined Coudé Laboratory. The vacuum removes air-column refractive-index variations from the optical path, while the thermal enclosure uses multiple actively controlled shells. This is not a single-loop control philosophy. It is a cascade in which each layer reduces the disturbance seen by the next layer.

The missing piece, from my perspective, is the frequency-domain response. An RMS temperature number does not show whether remaining thermal power lives on exposure timescales, nightly timescales, or seasonal timescales. For EXOhSPEC, the same systems language is useful: disturbance source, attenuation layer, residual optical-path response, and diagnostic feedback.

Resolving power is not a single number

The resolving-power maps are more useful than a headline R value. They show that resolving power changes with observing mode, detector position, order, slice, and wavelength. For precision work, resolution should be treated as a mapped quantity across the extracted format, not as one scalar label.

This is relevant to EXOhSPEC because a single quoted resolving power can hide position-dependent behaviour. A proper stability argument should eventually ask whether the detector region used for science extraction behaves like the region used for guiding or calibration diagnostics.

Throughput and efficiency: high resolution is not free

The efficiency plots show that throughput cannot be separated from resolution and seeing. HR mode reaches strong efficiency around the green part of the visible range, while the blue end drops substantially. UHR mode increases resolving power but makes aperture, seeing, slit loss, and fibre size more restrictive. The design trade-off is therefore not “higher resolving power is always better”.

For smaller instruments, this is a useful warning. If a design chases resolution without tracking throughput, radial-velocity precision can worsen because photon noise grows faster than information content improves. Resolution, throughput, fibre diameter, seeing, detector efficiency, and target type belong in the same error budget.

RV precision: short-term and long-term validation

The internal precision versus S/N plot links photon noise, spectral type, and information content. The seven-hour HD 85512 time series demonstrates short-term repeatability: the paper reports approximately 28 cm/s RMS for single 300 s exposures, close to the 25 cm/s photon-noise estimate, and binning five points reduces the scatter to roughly 8 cm/s.[1]

The long-baseline τ Ceti plots show the operational reality. The paper distinguishes data before and after the June 2019 fibre upgrade, with raw scatter changing from around 0.94 m/s before the intervention to around 0.72 m/s after it, and nightly-binned post-upgrade scatter around 0.58 m/s.[1] Short-term precision and long-baseline repeatability are not the same performance claim.

Calibration strategy: ThAr, Fabry-Pérot, and the LFC boundary

ThAr lamps provide absolute wavelength anchors, while a white-light Fabry-Pérot etalon provides dense drift monitoring. The laser-frequency comb was intended to provide a more complete calibration reference, but at the time of the performance paper it was not yet fully reliable or routine.[1]

Later calibration papers sharpen this section. Schmidt et al. tested a 34 GHz EOM comb at ESPRESSO and found that the source itself was stable, while calibration-unit injection geometry could introduce local wavelength-solution systematics.[2] Kreider et al. showed that Fabry-Pérot resonators can drift chromatically, which means an FP grid cannot always be treated as a rigid ruler.[3]

Open technical questions after this reading

  • Thermal transfer function: What is the frequency-domain transfer function from room to shell, vessel, bench, and wavelength solution?
  • Fibre-link root cause: The paper reports a fibre-link throughput deficiency that required repair. Was the dominant issue alignment, coating degradation, stress, modal behaviour, or another mechanism?
  • Calibration injection repeatability: If later comb tests reveal calibration-unit injection systematics, what tolerance should be assigned to the fibre-head or switching mechanism?
  • Scrambling residuals: What residual near-field or pupil variation remains at the pseudo-slit under different seeing and guiding conditions?
  • 4-UT stability: Has the 4-UT mode been demonstrated over the same long-baseline RV stability window as the 1-UT mode?
  • ThAr + FP cross-systematic: How does a sparse absolute ThAr solution interact with a dense FP grid that may have chromatic drift?

Connection to EXOhSPEC

This paper gives a checklist rather than a recipe. For EXOhSPEC, the writing should state where the disturbance enters, what each control or shielding layer attenuates, and which measured optical quantity proves that the correction is working. Temperature stability alone is not the final metric; the final metric is whether the optical path, spectral format, centroid, or wavelength solution becomes more stable.

The same point changes how TEC feedback should be presented. A TEC loop should not appear as an isolated control trick. It should be described as one element of a stability architecture: environmental sensing, thermal actuation, optical-path monitoring, image or echellogram tracking, calibration diagnostics, and a quantified residual error budget.
