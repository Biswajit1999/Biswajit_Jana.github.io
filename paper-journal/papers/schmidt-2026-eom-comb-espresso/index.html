A calibration paper about the delivery path, not only the comb

This page is a targeted reading of the Schmidt et al. test of a 34 GHz electro-optic modulation laser frequency comb on ESPRESSO. The most useful lesson is not simply that the comb is stable. The paper separates the quality of the light source from the stability of the calibration delivered to the detector.

That distinction matters because a frequency comb can be referenced to an absolute clock, but the spectrograph does not measure the clock directly. It measures fibre-fed, dispersed, sampled comb lines after injection, scrambling, optics, detector response, and line-spread-function modelling.

Why this follows the ESPRESSO performance paper

The ESPRESSO performance paper establishes the instrument as a vacuum-stabilised, fibre-fed EPRV benchmark with resolving powers around R ≈ 140,000 and R ≈ 190,000 in 1-UT modes and a demonstrated RV precision better than 25 cm/s over one night.[2] Schmidt et al. then ask a narrower calibration question: once an advanced frequency comb is inserted into the system, what limits the wavelength solution?

This makes the paper a natural follow-up. ESPRESSO already has a strong instrument architecture. The EOM-comb test shows that calibration accuracy can still be limited by how the calibration light is delivered and modelled, especially through the calibration unit, fibre feeds, echellogram structure, and non-Gaussian line-spread function.

What the 34 GHz EOM comb is testing

The paper tests a 34 GHz electro-optic modulation comb with ESPRESSO. The setup uses thin-film lithium-niobate waveguides for spectral broadening and harmonic generation, giving partial coverage of the infrared, visible and near-UV spectral ranges.[1]

The repetition spacing matters. Astronomical high-resolution spectrographs need comb modes that are separated enough to be resolved by the spectrograph, but dense enough to sample the wavelength solution. Earlier laser-comb discussions argued that mode spacings of roughly 5–30 GHz are typically needed for astronomical spectrographs; this 34 GHz implementation sits just above that practical range and is well matched to ESPRESSO-scale resolving power.[4]

Delivered spectra: background and line quality

The paper analyses spectrally diffuse background, line width and spectral quality of the delivered comb spectra. This is a useful emphasis because a comb spectrum is not automatically a clean wavelength ruler once it reaches the spectrograph. Background light, mode broadening, line asymmetry and detector sampling all affect how line centres are measured.

For a radial-velocity or wavelength-calibration pipeline, the relevant quantity is not only absolute frequency knowledge. It is the centre measured after the line passes through the instrument line-spread function and detector extraction. A source can have excellent metrological stability while still requiring careful modelling at the detector level.

Line-spread function: the model becomes part of the calibration

A central result is that excellent wavelength stability is only obtained when the non-Gaussian line-spread function is modelled accurately. This matches earlier ESPRESSO LSF work by Schmidt and Bouchy, which found that the ESPRESSO LSF is asymmetric, differs between slices and fibres, and varies along spectral orders. That work reduced discrepancies between independent wavelength solutions from about 50 m/s to around 10 m/s by using a more realistic LSF model.[3]

The message is important: the comb does not remove the need for a good instrument model. It reveals where the model is wrong. A pipeline that assumes an overly simple Gaussian line profile can turn a high-quality comb spectrum into a biased wavelength solution.

The calibration source can be clock-referenced, but the measured line centre is still an instrument-and-pipeline quantity. For EPRV work, LSF modelling is therefore part of calibration, not a post-processing detail.

The 15 m/s discrepancy: source stability versus injection geometry

The strongest result is the comparison between the EOM comb and the ESPRESSO facility comb. Schmidt et al. find strong local discrepancies in wavelength calibration accuracy of up to 15 m/s, correlated with the echellogram structure.[1] The important interpretation is that these discrepancies do not originate from the lasers themselves. The authors attribute them to misalignments in the ESPRESSO calibration unit.

This is the main technical lesson of the paper. A laser frequency comb can be stable, but the delivered calibration can still be biased by how light enters the instrument. Calibration-unit alignment, fibre injection, near-field/far-field differences and slice illumination can create wavelength-solution structure that is not a property of the comb source.

Earlier ESPRESSO calibration work had already identified wavelength-dependent discrepancies up to 24 m/s when comparing the default ThAr/Fabry-Pérot solution to an independent LFC solution.[5] The new EOM-comb work makes the point sharper by showing that source comparison can diagnose local instrument-delivery effects.

Stability: 17 cm/s after appropriate modelling

Despite the local calibration-unit discrepancies, the paper demonstrates excellent stability of the wavelength calibration, with a scatter of only 17 cm/s.[1] The condition is important: this level is reached when the non-Gaussian line-spread function is modelled accurately.

This number should therefore be read carefully. It is not a simple “comb equals 17 cm/s” statement. It is a combined result of source quality, delivered spectrum, line-profile modelling, wavelength-solution method and instrument state. That is why the paper is useful for reading after ESPRESSO: it shows how metrology and spectrograph optics meet in the actual data product.

Open technical questions after this reading

  • Calibration-unit tolerance: What mechanical or optical tolerance on calibration-unit alignment is required to keep local wavelength errors below 10 cm/s rather than many m/s?
  • Illumination monitoring: Can near-field and far-field monitoring of the calibration fibre detect the misalignment before it appears in the wavelength solution?
  • Time dependence: Are the local 15 m/s discrepancies stable maps that can be corrected, or do they drift with temperature, alignment, fibre stress or calibration-unit usage?
  • Transfer to science light: How closely does the calibration-light path reproduce the illumination state of the science fibres?
  • LSF generalisation: Does the non-Gaussian LSF model remain valid across time, observing mode, fibre, slice and detector location?
  • Operational complexity: Does the EOM-comb approach reduce long-term maintenance burden compared with facility comb systems, or does it move the complexity into data analysis and delivery diagnostics?

Calibration lesson for instrument-control work

The main lesson for smaller spectrograph projects is that calibration should be treated as a delivered optical state, not as a source specification. A stable source is necessary, but the calibration path must also be monitored. Fibre injection, illumination stability, line-profile modelling and detector extraction all enter the final wavelength solution.

This is useful when thinking about EXOhSPEC-style feedback work. A temperature loop can stabilise part of the instrument, but it does not replace calibration-path diagnostics. If the beam entering the spectrograph changes, or if the calibration and science paths are not equivalent, a thermally stable bench can still deliver a biased spectral format or wavelength solution.
