Targeted technical reading

What this note follows

The aim is not to choose a camera from one headline number. This reading asks what must be measured at pixel level, how camera settings change the result, and what additional evidence a precision-spectroscopy application would need.

Source findings

What the authors report

The paper compares Prime 95B, Sona-11, Sona-6, Ximea xiJ and Orca-Quest cameras. For the Orca-Quest at −20 °C with air cooling, Table 2 gives standard-mode read noise of 0.37 ± 0.09 e⁻ and dark current of 0.0067 ± 0.0003 e⁻/s; ultra-quiet mode gives 0.22 ± 0.07 e⁻ and 0.0198 ± 0.0008 e⁻/s. The photon-number-resolving mode has a different calibrated output and no conventional read-noise value in that table. Sampling was not uniform across every camera, and Sona-11 lacked the same pixel-wise analysis. This is imaging characterisation, not EPRV qualification. [1]

Interpretation and technical reasoning

A detector noise budget needs an estimator

For independent noise terms in a simple aperture, a schematic variance is S + B + nₚ(Dt + σᵣ²), with source and sky electron counts S and B, nₚ pixels, dark rate D, exposure t and read noise σᵣ. This is a bookkeeping model, not a complete camera specification. Correlated noise and calibration uncertainty require additional terms.

Changing pixel size also changes sampling and the number of pixels used by an estimator. A lower per-pixel read noise may not give a lower extracted-spectrum variance if the same optical profile spans many more pixels. Comparisons should hold the intended measurement and sampling requirement fixed.

Interpretation and technical reasoning

Why maps matter more than a brochure value

A wavelength centroid is a weighted combination of pixel signals. If pixel response errors vary across the line profile, their effect depends on where the profile lands. A detector can perform well for total flux while introducing a small position-dependent centroid bias.

I would preserve raw frames, acquisition settings, calibration maps and masking decisions together. Reprocessing then becomes possible when a noisy tail, unstable pixel population or amplifier pattern is found later. A single corrected image without that provenance is difficult to audit.

Interpretation and technical reasoning

The boundary between low noise and accurate spectroscopy

Sub-electron read noise is valuable, but it does not establish intra-pixel uniformity, stable pixel geometry, timing accuracy or a negligible flux-dependent centroid shift. Those are separate observables with separate tests.

Nor should a proprietary integer-valued output be treated as an ordinary analogue frame without checking its transfer function. Quantisation, calibration and saturation can change the interpretation of variance. The data model must follow the actual output mode.

Proposed work · not performed

A spectroscopy-specific validation plan

Flux sweep: move an unresolved, stable line through a range of brightnesses while holding illumination geometry fixed. Inspect centroid and width residuals as well as integrated flux linearity.

Sub-pixel scan: translate the line slowly across pixel phase and repeat at several detector locations. Separate optical motion from detector response using an independent position reference.

Repeatability: repeat after power cycling, mode changes and temperature cycles. Use a fixed reference reduction first, then quantify how much recalibration is required.

Covariance: examine row, column and temporal residual correlations. A per-pixel standard deviation does not capture noise shared by many pixels.

Interpretation and technical reasoning

Connection to instrumentation work

For a precision spectrograph I would write a detector acceptance test around the final scientific estimator, not around camera specifications alone. The useful deliverable is a reproducible link from pixel behaviour to extraction and wavelength error.

This also fits the wider control-system theme: stabilise the hardware, record its state, and verify the result out of loop. A quiet electronic readout does not remove the need to test the optical measurement.

Extended reading · methods, assumptions and interpretation

Following the full technical argument

The experiment measures a population of pixels, not one camera number

A CMOS array contains many local electronic response chains. A global mean can look excellent even if a small subset of pixels is unstable or if row-level correlations remain. That is why gain, bias, read-noise and dark-response distributions need to be followed through the paper rather than reduced to a product ranking.

Conversion gain links the digital output to electron counts. In a shot-noise-dominated regime, variance and mean should obey a predictable relation once the convention for gain is fixed. An incorrect conversion changes every downstream electron-based claim. Mean–variance plots are consequently a calibration diagnostic as well as a convenient way to quote gain.

Repeated data sets are especially useful because they ask whether the calibration map transfers beyond one acquisition. A map that fits one exposure sequence can hide source instability or readout-state changes. The paper flags unequal sampling among cameras; I would not convert those comparisons into a precise vendor league table.

Read noise, dark current, quantum efficiency and linearity answer different questions

Read noise describes variation associated with reading out a pixel, whereas dark current accumulates with exposure time. Their relative importance changes with cadence and illumination. A slow low-noise mode is therefore not automatically optimal for a rapid measurement, and an attractive bias-frame result does not establish low-noise performance during a long exposure.

Quantum efficiency is a photon-conversion measurement with its own reference detector and optical path. The front window matters: a combined camera response must not be labelled a bare-sensor response without correcting for transmission. The reliable wavelength range of a revised measurement should also be respected rather than interpolated into unsupported ultraviolet performance.

Linearity must be read as residual versus signal, with saturation and output processing visible. An average linearity score can conceal a local response bend at precisely the signal level used by a scientific estimator. For photon-number-resolving output, the calibration algorithm changes the distribution being measured; it should not be treated as an ordinary analogue mode with a familiar noise statistic.

Why the appendix changes the strength of the conclusion

The appendix is not only extra camera plots. It contains warning signs that qualify comparisons: incomplete pixel-wise calibration in one case, unusual gain populations in another and a mean–variance inconsistency requiring further investigation. Those are reasons to inspect the measurement procedure, not reasons to infer a violation of photon statistics.

A variance below a naïve shot-noise expectation can arise from processing, correlations, gain conventions or an inadequate experimental reference. The correct next step is an independent illumination and acquisition check. It is not to present the camera as beating the fundamental randomness of incident photons.

For a spectrograph, the next validation layer is the movement of a line or spectrum over these calibrated pixels. A precise integrated flux does not guarantee an unbiased centroid. I would propagate measured response maps through realistic extraction weights and test brightness, pixel phase, detector position and readout state before claiming precision-RV suitability.
