Paper Note 02  ·  ePRV / Radial Velocity

Uncovering Hidden Systematics in Extreme-Precision Radial Velocity Measurements

Zhao, Fischer, Szymkowiak, Brewer & Llama · ApJS, 2026 · arXiv:2601.02296

AuthorsLily L. Zhao, Debra A. Fischer, Andrew E. Szymkowiak, John M. Brewer, Joe Llama
JournalThe Astrophysical Journal Supplement Series (ApJS), 2026
arXivarXiv:2601.02296
DOI10.3847/1538-4365/ae3088
InstrumentEXPRES echelle spectrograph at the Lowell Discovery Telescope (LDT)
ThemeePRV / Radial Velocity
Core idea

The central problem

EXPRES is a stabilised, fibre-fed echelle spectrograph at the Lowell Discovery Telescope, built around a rectangular optical fibre that approximates a slit, delivering R ~ 137,000 across ~490–730 nm. Wavelength calibration comes from a Menlo Systems laser frequency comb (LFC), giving demonstrated calibration stability of 4–7 cm/s. On-sky precision reaches 25–30 cm/s at SNR > 200.

By any reasonable measure, this is an extremely stable instrument — and yet, buried in seven years of radial velocity data, there is a coherent instrumental drift with a trough-to-peak amplitude of 2.8 m/s that the standard pipeline never flagged. That is large enough to mimic or bury a super-Earth. Zhao et al. track it down, characterise it, and demonstrate a correction that reduces the solar RV trend from 1.32 m/s RMS to 0.43 m/s — a 67% improvement.

The trend only became visible after accumulating enough data to do multi-target temporal binning: stacking the RVs of twelve chromospherically quiet survey stars (F9–K3 spectral type, selected for RMS < 2.5 m/s) and applying an 11-day median filter. Astrophysical variability averages out while the common instrumental trend is preserved. Confirmation that the trend is truly instrumental comes from comparing EXPRES and NEID solar RVs: differencing their co-binned solar data removes the shared stellar signal, leaving the EXPRES–NEID instrumental difference — and the same dip appears, peaking around early 2022 when EXPRES experienced unusually large temperature excursions.

Two new diagnostic tools are developed beyond standard telemetry. First, an LFC Line Bisector Span (LBS): instrument profile (IP) asymmetry is measured by computing the bisector span of 20 LFC lines near 5840 Å at the 30th and 90th percentile flux levels. Because LFC modes are far narrower than the spectrograph resolving power, each mode is effectively a delta function convolved with the IP — a clean probe of IP shape changes at the 10⁻⁶ nm scale. Second, 2D echellogram position tracking: LFC line centres are fitted on the raw 2D detector image before extraction, in three echelle orders, tracking shifts, scalings, and skews in both the dispersion (x) and cross-dispersion (y) directions.

The standard 1D wavelength solution absorbed x-direction shifts already. It was the y-direction — cross-dispersion — that was the most diagnostically informative signal the pipeline had never looked at.
Why I selected this

Relevance to EXOhSPEC

This paper is almost exactly the kind of analysis I expect to need to run on EXOhSPEC data, scaled down in complexity but identical in structure. The three RTD sensors in EXPRES — one on the optical bench inside the vacuum enclosure, one on the vessel exterior, one on the seismic isolation slab — are the precise hardware hierarchy I am working with in my own thermal control and monitoring setup.

The finding that milli-Kelvin-level temperature variations correlate with m/s-level RV drifts validates the premise of close environmental monitoring. More specifically, the decomposition into optical-bench, vessel-exterior, and slab readings allows you to trace where the thermal disturbance enters the system and how efficiently the passive and active stabilisation rejects it — exactly the diagnostic structure I need for EXOhSPEC.

The 2D echellogram drift result is what I keep thinking about. For EXOhSPEC, which is a ground-based fibre-fed spectrograph experiencing temperature and pressure cycling, this is a concrete argument for designing the data reduction pipeline to monitor echellogram geometry from day one — not just dispersion-direction calibration residuals.

Key concept

The multi-dimensional weighted least-squares correction

The core of the correction is a generalised least-squares regression (Equations 4–6 of the paper):

Equations 4–6 · Zhao et al. 2026
Design matrix: X = [ x_{1,1} x_{1,2} ... x_{1,T} ] (N obs, T tracers) Coefficients: β̂ = (Xᵀ C⁻¹ X)⁻¹ (Xᵀ C⁻¹ Y) (Eq. 5) Correction: ŷ = β̂ · X (Eq. 6)

X is the N×T design matrix (rows = observations, columns = normalised instrumental tracers: 3 temperature RTDs, echellogram x/y shifts, scalings, skews, and LBS — T = 9 total). Y is the N×1 vector of 11-day median-binned RVs. C is the diagonal covariance matrix. β̂ is the regression coefficient vector. The correction ŷ is subtracted from the original RVs.

Several implementation choices matter. All tracers are normalised to zero mean and unit variance before fitting — this keeps the design matrix well-conditioned. The dependent variable Y is the median-binned RV from multi-target stacking, not individual stellar measurements, so astrophysical noise (uncorrelated between targets) averages out while the instrumental trend (correlated across all targets) is preserved. Separate models are fitted for nighttime data and for the Δ solar time series, with leave-one-out validation. Temperature tracers are binned in 4-hour windows; empirical tracers daily — reflecting the physics of how quickly each diagnostic responds.

What makes this approach powerful is not the mathematics — it is standard GLS — but the physical logic: the instrument is treated as a linear system whose RV output is a linear combination of observable state variables. This is the same approach used in control system identification and is directly analogous to the model-based feedforward correction in a well-designed feedback loop.

Open questions

What I want to understand better

  • Spatial thermal gradients: The correction uses temperature telemetry from three sensors but treats each as a scalar time series. Thermal gradients within the instrument — between the collimator, echelle grating, and camera — are not mapped. If there is a differential thermal gradient across the optical bench, the single optical-bench RTD could be misleading. What is the spatial uniformity of the thermal environment inside the EXPRES vacuum enclosure?
  • Wavelength dependence of LBS: The LBS is measured from 20 LFC lines in a 3 Å window at 5840 Å. EXPRES covers ~490–730 nm across ~37 echelle orders. If IP asymmetry varies with field angle or wavelength — as expected from optical aberrations — a single narrow-region LBS does not represent the full detector. The paper notes that LBS 'changes slightly across the detector' but does not show the spatial dependence in detail.
  • Non-linearity and hysteresis: The correction model is linear with no cross-terms. If the IP asymmetry is a non-linear function of temperature — for example, if camera lenses have a nonlinear thermal expansion producing hysteresis in the tilt angle — residuals after correction would show asymmetric behaviour during temperature rise vs. fall. Was this tested?
  • ρ CrB stellar activity: The 262-day signal removal is compelling, but I want confirmation that this period has been ruled out as stellar activity. A stellar rotation period near ~250 days is unlikely for a G0 star, but differential rotation aliases or activity cycles could produce signals on these timescales. Were S-index, Hα, and CCF bisector FWHM checked for the same period?
  • CTE degradation: Charge transfer efficiency effects in the CCD are discussed but not corrected. The 7-year baseline is long enough that CTE degradation could contribute a long-term trend in the extracted wavelength zero point that neither the LFC calibration nor the telemetry-based correction would capture.
Research gaps

Gaps I noticed in the paper

  • Correlated tracers: The correction model treats all T = 9 tracers as independent columns in the design matrix. But temperature affects both echellogram position and IP asymmetry simultaneously — they may be correlated responses to the same physical cause. Including them as uncorrelated columns double-counts explanatory power. A PCA of the tracer time series before regression would clarify whether independent information is truly being captured.
  • Order-by-order LBS: IP asymmetry is characterised in a single 3 Å window. An order-by-order LBS analysis — even with fewer comb lines per order — would give a wavelength-resolved picture of IP variation and potentially allow a spectrally-dependent correction.
  • No independent mechanical evidence: The hypothesised physical mechanism — thermal tilts of camera barrel lenses — is speculative. No autocollimator or Hartmann test was performed during the 2022 temperature excursion. The root cause therefore remains unconfirmed, which matters because different mechanisms require different engineering solutions.
  • Forward-looking activity overlap: The paper does not address what happens when stellar activity signals overlap in period with the instrumental trend. Any star with a rotation period or activity cycle near 260–280 days would be particularly difficult to analyse, since the instrumental power could amplify or suppress genuine activity signals.
Connection to my work

What this means for EXOhSPEC

The three-sensor RTD configuration (optical bench inside vacuum, vessel exterior, seismic slab) is directly applicable to EXOhSPEC. What this paper crystallises is the strategy behind multi-point temperature monitoring: you need sensors at different thermal coupling distances from the optics to resolve the transfer function of your thermal control system. The optical-bench reading tells you what the optics experienced; the vessel-exterior reading tells you what the thermal controller delivered to the boundary; the slab reading tells you what disturbances the isolation layer passed through. For my TEC-based thermal control loop, the analogous hierarchy would be: TEC cold side (measuring what the optical bench receives), TEC hot side (measuring heat rejection), and enclosure exterior (measuring the ambient boundary condition).

The 2D echellogram drift diagnostic is the most directly actionable result for EXOhSPEC. I had not thought explicitly about monitoring cross-dispersion position stability as a calibration metric — I had mentally assumed that the 1D wavelength solution would capture most of the relevant instrument state. This paper demonstrates that it does not. I should add echellogram y-position monitoring to the diagnostic pipeline from the start, using LFC or etalon reference frames, and not wait seven years to discover it.

The injection-recovery result and the ρ CrB false planet are a cautionary lesson about claims made on incomplete baselines. This argues for two things: building solar or reference-star monitoring from the beginning of operations rather than retrofitting it; and being conservative about signals with periods comparable to the timescale of the instrument's dominant thermal variation.

★ Solid — Recommended

Overall assessment

This is the kind of paper the EPRV field needs more of. It is not a planet detection, not a new instrument design, and not a fundamentally new statistical method. What it does is methodically document a real instrumental systematic in a real high-quality dataset, develop a practical correction framework, and demonstrate the correction's reliability through injection-recovery tests and a concrete case study in ρ CrB. The linear regression approach is deliberately simple and interpretable — easy to verify and easy to extend. The discussion section is notably self-aware about limitations, which makes it more credible than papers that present a correction and move on. For anyone building a new spectrograph, this paper makes a strong empirical case for solar feeds, LFC-based IP diagnostics, 2D echellogram monitoring, and multi-year baseline analysis as non-optional parts of the precision RV toolkit.

Brown dwarf number density in the JWST COSMOS-Web field

Amos Y.-A. Chen, Tomotsugu Goto et al. · PASA, Vol. 42, 2025 · arXiv:2503.18510

AuthorsAmos Y.-A. Chen, Tomotsugu Goto et al.
JournalPublications of the Astronomical Society of Australia (PASA), Vol. 42, 2025
arXivarXiv:2503.18510
Survey fieldCOSMOS-Web (0.243 deg² DR0.5 release)
InstrumentJWST/NIRCam — filters F115W, F150W, F277W, F444W
ThemeBrown Dwarfs
Core idea

The central problem

This paper goes after a question that sounds deceptively simple: how many late-T and Y dwarfs exist at kiloparsec distances in the Milky Way, and what does their number density tell us about the low-mass end of the stellar initial mass function? The difficulty is purely observational. Brown dwarfs in the T/Y regime — effective temperatures between roughly 700 and 1300 K — emit almost nothing shortward of 1 micron. In the near-infrared they are faint enough that all-sky surveys like 2MASS, WISE, and UKIDSS could only detect them reliably within about 20 pc of the Sun. At 1 kpc, the same T8 dwarf would be ~10,000 times fainter in flux. JWST's NIRCam changes that, and COSMOS-Web — currently the largest contiguous JWST survey field at 0.54 deg², with the DR0.5 release covering 0.243 deg² — gives this study genuine statistical leverage.

The colour selection approach is what I find genuinely clever here. Brown dwarfs cooler than ~1300 K have a distinctive V-shaped spectral energy distribution: they peak around the 1.1-micron J-band window and again beyond 4 microns, but they have a deep trough at 2.7 microns because water and methane absorption is maximal there. JWST/NIRCam's filter set straddles this feature: F115W sits in the J-band flux peak, F277W sits in the absorption trough, and F444W sits redward where the SED rises again. Chen et al. formalise this into two photometric colour inequalities that select objects with this V-shaped curvature, apply morphological point-source cuts using SExtractor, and then run MCMC-based SED fitting against three atmospheric model grids — Sonora-Bobcat, ATMO2020++, and LOWZ — to derive Teff, log g, and metallicity for each candidate.

The result is 25 T-dwarf and 2 Y-dwarf candidates located between 0.3 and 4 kpc — more than any earlier JWST brown dwarf search. Their cumulative number counts are consistent with a standard double-exponential Galactic disk model. Three candidates with archival HST detections yield transverse velocities of 12–17 km/s, kinematically consistent with the Galactic thin disk. Temperature-binned number densities come out in the 10⁻⁶ pc⁻³ range for the coolest bins, in agreement with predictions from solar-neighbourhood surveys scaled to larger Galactic volumes.

The same T8 dwarf visible to WISE within 20 pc is detectable in COSMOS-Web at 1 kpc. That 50-fold reach into the Galactic disk is what makes JWST qualitatively different for brown dwarf population statistics.
Key concept

The colour selection criteria — Equations 1 and 2

The elegance of this paper is in how much atmospheric physics is encoded into two photometric inequalities that are computationally trivial to evaluate on a 113,456-source catalogue:

Equations 1 & 2 · Chen et al. 2025
Eq. 1: F115W − F277W + 1 < F277W − F444W Eq. 2: F277W − F444W > 0.9 where F115W, F277W, F444W are AB magnitudes in JWST/NIRCam filters centred at 1.154, 2.762, and 4.404 microns respectively.

Equation 2 — the slope condition. F277W − F444W > 0.9 means the source is noticeably brighter at 4.4 μm than at 2.8 μm. A colour of 0.9 corresponds to the source being roughly 2.3× brighter in flux density at F444W than at F277W — the flux rise out of the water absorption trough into the M-band window, which opens when both CH₄ and H₂O are effectively saturated. The 0.9 threshold selects objects cooler than Teff ~ 1300 K; warmer L dwarfs generally do not satisfy it.

Equation 1 — the curvature condition. Rearranging gives: (F277W − F444W) − (F115W − F277W) > 1, or equivalently F115W − 2×F277W + F444W > 1. This is the discrete second difference of the SED sampled at three wavelength points — a photometric curvature measure. A large positive value means the SED is concave upward at F277W: it dips below the line connecting the F115W and F444W flux points. Requiring this curvature to exceed 1 mag means the 2.7-μm flux must be suppressed by more than ~2.5× relative to a linear interpolation between J-band and M-band — encoding the temperature-dependent molecular opacities of cool atmospheres.

Together, the two inequalities define a region in colour space that is physically motivated and relatively unambiguous. Eq. 2 alone would pass reddened stars; Eq. 1 alone at a lower threshold would allow through objects with modest SED dips. The combination is more discriminating than either alone — though contamination from high-redshift Lyman-break galaxies remains a concern, which is why the morphological point-source cuts are essential.

Why I selected this

Connection to instrumentation thinking

I picked this up primarily because of the filter-based selection methodology. The colour criteria essentially measure the photometric depth of the 2.7-μm water absorption feature by differencing magnitudes across three bands. That a ±0.1 mag uncertainty in F277W − F444W determines whether a source is classified as a T dwarf or rejected as a red compact galaxy is not so different in spirit from how a 1 m/s uncertainty in a radial velocity measurement determines whether you see a planet or noise. In both cases, the limiting factor is instrument stability translating into measurement fidelity.

The multi-model SED fitting and associated parameter degeneracies also connect to problems I think about in EPRV analysis. Teff, log g, and metallicity shape the photometric colours in partially degenerate ways — structurally analogous to how stellar activity, instrumental drift, and planetary Doppler shifts degenerate when working near the noise floor. The inclusion of ATMO2020++ and LOWZ models to handle subsolar-metallicity thick-disk brown dwarfs is a good example of using domain knowledge to break a degeneracy — the same principle as using a chromatic activity indicator to separate astrophysical and instrumental contributions in RV data.

Open questions

What I want to understand better

  • Morphological completeness at the faint limit: Star/galaxy separation uses CLASS_STAR, FLUX_RADIUS, and SPREAD_MODEL from SExtractor. At AB ~ 27.5 in F277W, NIRCam's PSF FWHM at 2.8 μm is ~0.09 arcsec on a 0.03 arcsec/pix scale. At marginal S/N, PSF wings blend into noise and SPREAD_MODEL becomes noisy. I would want a quantitative completeness-vs-contamination curve as a function of AB magnitude, not just visual inspection of faint candidates.
  • Inter-model scatter in Teff: Three atmospheric model grids are used independently, and reported Teff comes from whichever gives the best-fit χ². If Sonora-Bobcat gives Teff = 1100 K and ATMO2020++ gives 950 K for the same photometry, that is a different spectral type and a different number density bin. The paper does not appear to quantify inter-model scatter, which seems like a significant gap in the systematic error budget.
  • HST-to-JWST astrometric registration: Transverse velocity estimates rely on the astrometric cross-match between HST/ACS epochs and COSMOS-Web. The quoted uncertainties of ~5 km/s suggest good precision, but what is the systematic floor from absolute astrometric registration between two instruments with different PSF models, pixel scales, and filter sets over several years?
  • Double-exponential disk model sensitivity: The assumed scale heights in the model have fixed values. For a survey reaching 4 kpc, cumulative counts are sensitive to the assumed thin/thick disk decomposition. 'Consistent with the standard model' is only informative if we know how tightly the data actually constrain the model parameters, rather than just lying within the 1-sigma envelope.
Research gaps

Gaps I noticed in the paper

  • No spectroscopic confirmation: Every classification is photometric and model-dependent. Without at least one JWST/NIRSpec spectrum showing the water and methane absorption bands directly, both the T/Y classification and the distance estimate rest entirely on the SED fit, and the headline number densities inherit that uncertainty completely.
  • No quantitative completeness assessment: Morphological star/galaxy separation is validated qualitatively by visual inspection, but there is no completeness assessment as a function of AB magnitude. For a survey operating near its detection limit, the incompleteness fraction as a function of magnitude matters directly when converting raw source counts into number densities.
  • Metallicity grid mismatch: Sonora-Bobcat is solar metallicity only, while ATMO2020++ and LOWZ extend to [Fe/H] = −1. For objects potentially in the thick disk or halo at 2–4 kpc, using the wrong grid could bias Teff by 100–200 K — enough to shift a source into a different temperature bin. The paper does not cross-validate by running all three grids on a common subset of candidates.
  • Binary fraction not discussed: Binary brown dwarfs at these temperatures would appear photometrically as a single unresolved source roughly 0.75 mag brighter than a single object of the same Teff. If the binary fraction at late-T/Y types is 10–20%, the number density and temperature distribution are biased at a comparable level.
  • Spatially varying sensitivity: The area incompleteness from the missing F444W chip in the A8 field and from depth variations across mosaic tiles is treated as a single fixed-area correction. A spatially varying sensitivity map would be more rigorous, especially since the faintest candidates are both the most numerous and the most sensitive to this effect.
Connection to my work

What this means for EPRV and instrumentation thinking

This paper is not about spectrographs or radial velocities, but the underlying measurement philosophy maps directly onto what I deal with in EXOhSPEC work. The COSMOS-Web selection is fundamentally a precision photometric discrimination problem: can your instrument's calibration and stability be trusted enough to make an astrophysical classification at the photon-noise limit? NIRCam is cryogenically cooled in space — the PSF is stable, there is no atmospheric dispersion, and thermal drift is effectively zero. That is why F277W − F444W can be trusted to a few hundredths of a magnitude for AB ~ 27 sources. In the ground-based EPRV world, I am asking the structurally identical question: can my wavelength solution and instrument profile be held stable enough to detect a 1 m/s Doppler shift against a background of stellar jitter, atmospheric dispersion, and thermal drift? Different numbers, the same root problem of instrument stability as the limiting factor for measurement precision.

The proper motion analysis using multi-year HST-to-JWST astrometric baselines is a smaller but concrete reminder of what stable reference frames enable. Every physical measurement requires a stable, well-characterised reference frame, and understanding its drift is as important as understanding the signal itself — a principle that is as true in spectrograph calibration as it is in multi-epoch astrometry.

★ Solid — First step, not definitive

Overall assessment

This is a methodologically clean survey paper that does exactly what it sets out to do: extend the brown dwarf census to kiloparsec distances and convert those counts into temperature-binned number densities that can be compared with Galactic disk models. The use of the COSMOS-Web field gives the result more statistical weight than earlier JWST brown dwarf searches, and the deliberate inclusion of subsolar-metallicity model grids shows real thought about which Galactic populations are being probed. The major limitation is that everything rests on photometric classification without spectroscopic validation, and the systematic uncertainties from inter-model scatter and morphological completeness are not fully quantified. As a first measurement of T/Y dwarf number densities at kpc scales, this is a genuinely useful data point for the low-mass IMF and Galactic structure communities — a solid observational first step that sets up the follow-up spectroscopy that will eventually turn these candidates into a clean, confirmed population.

Why I selected this

Filter-based precision selection at the photon-noise limit — structurally analogous to the instrument stability problem in EPRV. Also directly relevant to my Halo T dwarf work with Prof. Pinfield.

Main problem

Measuring T/Y dwarf number densities at kiloparsec distances from the Sun. Requires detecting objects ~10,000× fainter in flux than the same dwarfs within 20 pc, only made possible by JWST/NIRCam.

Instrument / dataset

JWST/NIRCam. COSMOS-Web field, 0.243 deg² DR0.5 release. Filters F115W, F150W, F277W, F444W. 113,456 band-merged sources. Three model grids: Sonora-Bobcat, ATMO2020++, LOWZ.

Main result

25 T-dwarf + 2 Y-dwarf candidates at 0.3–4 kpc. Temperature-binned number densities ~10⁻⁶ pc⁻³. Consistent with double-exponential Galactic disk model. Kinematically consistent with thin disk (12–17 km/s transverse velocity).

Key equations

Eq. 1: F115W − F277W + 1 < F277W − F444W (curvature, SED second difference > 1 mag). Eq. 2: F277W − F444W > 0.9 (slope, flux rise from trough to M-band).

Figure focus

Colour-colour selection diagram, representative SED fits (3 panels), number density vs. temperature bins, cumulative N(>F444W) counts, HST proper motion kinematics.

Open questions for future reading

NIRSpec spectroscopic follow-up of candidate confirmation. Inter-model Teff systematics quantification. Binary fraction bias. Spatially varying incompleteness correction.
